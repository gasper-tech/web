Markets & Macro · this week

Japan's record yen rescue keeps slipping away

Japan has spent more than ¥25 trillion propping up the yen this year, a record. Over the summer alone it spent ¥15.4 trillion, including the first joint operation with the US since 1998. It did not stick. In mid-November the yen slid back past 160 per dollar, the line Tokyo has been defending. This week a Bank of Japan board member, Naoki Tamura, said rates should rise faster, towards about 2% from 1.25% today. Markets now lean towards a hike on 18 December.

How intervention works: the finance ministry sells dollars from its reserves and buys yen, and the price jumps. But it is a sandbag, not a dam. Money goes where it earns more, and US rates sit far above Japan's. So investors borrow cheap yen, park it in dollars, and the currency drifts back. Only a smaller rate gap, through Japanese hikes or US cuts, turns the tide; spending reserves just buys time.

Why it matters
Japanese savers are among the world's biggest buyers of foreign bonds, European ones included. If Japan's rates climb, some of that money heads home, which can nudge up borrowing costs here. And sudden yen jumps have shaken global stock markets before, so 18 December is worth a glance.

Why the climate summit ended with no deal

The UN climate summit in Antalya, COP31, closed on Sunday 22 November without its headline text, the "Antalya Pact". It failed twice, from opposite sides. In week two, the Arab Group and Russia rejected a draft that targeted specific fuels and set a goal of electricity supplying 35% of final energy by 2035. The presidency cut those parts. Then the EU, the UK, Norway and Panama rejected the final draft because it no longer mentioned moving away from fossil fuels.

How COP decisions work: there is no vote. Countries have used draft rules of procedure since 1995, but never agreed on rule 42, the one setting voting majorities. So every decision needs consensus, which in practice means no objection the chair can ignore. That turns any determined bloc into a veto player, and the same text can be killed for being too strong or too weak. Smaller decisions, such as on carbon markets under Article 6, were adopted separately and still stand.

Why it matters
Climate Action says this is the first COP since Copenhagen in 2009 to end without an overarching political deal. Pledges like the 35% electricity goal now stay voluntary side deals among willing countries. And the voting-rules question has been put on the agenda again for COP32.

Sources: Xinhua: COP31 fails to reach consensus on Antalya Pact · Climate Action: COP31 ends without a political deal · Legal Response International: COP rules of procedure and consensus

Medicine · recent research

Heart trouble starts drifting 30 years early

Heart attacks feel sudden. The numbers behind them are not. A study in JAMA Network Open this month pooled four long-running cohorts covering 113,612 adults with no previous heart attack or stroke, followed for a median of almost 19 years. The team tracked blood pressure, cholesterol and blood markers over time, then looked back from the moment a heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death occurred.

The trajectories of people who later had an event began to split from everyone else's up to three decades earlier. Thirty years out, women who went on to have an event averaged a systolic pressure of 121 mm Hg, against 117 in those who stayed well. Small, but it widened to about 7 mm Hg by the end. A strain hormone called NT-proBNP only diverged in the last decade or so. The limits: it is observational, it describes group averages rather than forecasting any one person, and it cannot show that acting earlier changes the outcome.

Why it matters
A reading of 121 looks perfectly fine, which is the point: the slow drift is easy to ignore in your thirties and forties. Knowing your numbers and watching their direction, not just whether they cross a line, may be worth more than it seems. General information, not medical advice.

History · explained

The shipwrecked gearbox that tracked the heavens

In 1900, Greek sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the island of Antikythera stumbled on a Roman-era shipwreck. Among the treasures hauled up in 1900–1901 were some unloved lumps of corroded bronze. Then, in May 1902, a gear wheel was spotted in one of them at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Credit usually goes to the archaeologist Valerios Stais, though newspapers at the time said his cousin Spyridon noticed it first.

Gears, in antiquity? Derek de Solla Price made the case in Gears from the Greeks (1974) that it was a calendar computer. In 2005 the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project scanned the 82 surviving fragments with X-ray CT, and in 2006 Tony Freeth and colleagues published the results in Nature. About 30 bronze gears survive. Turn a handle and the device showed the Sun, the Moon and its phases, and predicted eclipses using the saros, a cycle of 223 lunar months. A clever pin-and-slot even made the Moon speed up and slow down, as it does in the sky. One dial tracked the four-year cycle of the Olympic games.

A 2021 model from a UCL team argues it showed the planets too, though most of that part is lost. It was probably built in the 2nd or 1st century BC; experts still argue over exactly when. Nothing remotely as complex survives from the next thousand years or more. It sits, in pieces, in Athens.

Why it matters
It shows ancient Greek engineering was far more advanced than the surviving texts let on, and that a whole technology can vanish almost without trace.

Science · explained

Optical illusions catch your brain guessing

Your eyes send your brain patchy, ambiguous signals, not a photograph. In 1867 the physicist Hermann von Helmholtz called this “unconscious inference”: what you see is the brain's best guess about what's out there. Usually the guess is brilliant. Illusions are where you catch it guessing.

Take Edward Adelson's checker-shadow illusion, published at MIT in 1995. Two squares on a checkerboard, one in shadow, look plainly different. They are exactly the same grey. Your brain knows shadows darken things, so it corrects for the shadow, and you see the corrected version. The Müller-Lyer illusion (1889) plays a similar trick with arrowheads, making equal lines look unequal. In the 1960s Segall, Campbell and Herskovits reported that people from less “carpentered”, right-angled surroundings were fooled less, but that idea is now hotly disputed. Then, in 2015, came #TheDress. Blue and black, or white and gold? The leading explanation is that people assumed different lighting. Assume bluish daylight and your brain subtracts the blue, leaving white and gold.

Knowing an illusion is a trick does not make it stop working. The squares still look different after you've measured them. That suggests parts of perception run largely on their own, without checking what you consciously know. Your brain is quietly deciding what reality looks like, and it doesn't ask you first.

Why it matters
Illusions aren't glitches: they expose the assumptions your brain uses every second to turn messy signals into a stable world.

Mental Model · explained

Why one story beats a thousand numbers

Tell people about Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali, and they reach for their wallets. In a 2007 study by Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, people who read her story gave an average of $2.38 of their $5 fee. Those shown statistics about millions of hungry people gave $1.14. Add the statistics to Rokia's story and giving fell to $1.43. Vivid stories are easy to recall, so they feel common and true. That's the availability heuristic.

Stories also hide what's missing. In the famous tale, the statistician Abraham Wald told the US military to armour bombers where returning planes had no bullet holes, because planes hit there never came home. The popular version is tidied up, but the lesson holds: survivorship bias lets the winners write the dataset. An anecdote tells you something happened; it can't tell you how often, or why.

So evidence has a ladder: anecdote, case series, observational studies, randomised trials, then systematic reviews that pool many trials. And "the plural of anecdote is not data"? The political scientist Raymond Wolfinger said roughly the opposite in the late 1960s: "The plural of anecdote is data." Both are half right. Stories collected carefully become data. Cherry-picked ones stay stories.

Why it matters
When a story moves you, ask which rung of the ladder it sits on, and whose story you're not hearing. Then go and find the boring number.

Philosophy
“A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence.”
David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X “Of Miracles”, Part I · 1748

The line comes from the essay on miracles in Hume's Enquiry. His point is that belief comes in degrees. Things backed by uniform experience, like the sun rising, deserve near-certainty. Things backed by mixed experience deserve less. When the evidence pulls both ways, you weigh it and take the difference. Testimony is just one more kind of evidence, and it has to be weighed like the rest. Hume used this to argue that a report of a miracle almost never outweighs the experience against it. Carl Sagan later boiled it down to "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence."

Book of the week · Fridays

Longitude, by Dava Sobel

In the early 1700s, sailors could work out how far north they were from the sun, but not how far east or west, so ships kept finding rocks the hard way. In 1714 Parliament offered up to £20,000 for a fix. The astronomers bet on reading the moon and stars. John Harrison, a self-taught country carpenter, bet on a clock that could keep time on a rolling ship. Dava Sobel's 1995 bestseller follows the decades he spent building one, only to find that the hardest part was getting the experts to believe him. It's under 200 pages and reads like a thriller.

Read it if
you like a story about one stubborn outsider taking on the establishment, and winning (eventually).
