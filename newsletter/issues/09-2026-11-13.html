Markets & Macro · this week

America's inflation report lost its headline number

On Tuesday the US published its October inflation report, and the most famous number in it was missing. Prices rose 0.4% in the month, above the 0.3% forecast, and core prices (food and energy stripped out) rose 0.3%. But there was no annual rate. Statisticians could not say how much prices rose over the year, because they never collected October 2025 prices: the government was shut down. The last annual reading, for September, was 3.7%.

How the annual rate works: it simply compares this month's price index with the same month a year earlier. Remove one end of the ruler and there is nothing to measure. Economists are filling the gap with their own estimates, which is guesswork with better spreadsheets. The timing is awkward. The Fed meets on 8–9 December, and the November report lands on 10 December, a day too late. So markets, now betting on a December hike, are steering by monthly numbers alone.

Why it matters
Headline statistics look solid, but each rests on a long chain of routine data collection. When one link breaks, the damage can surface a year later. Interest-rate decisions, and the dollar, bond yields and euro exchange rates that follow them, then get made with blurrier vision.

World · this week

Spain's big strike, and the fight over its size

On Wednesday Spain had its first general strike in 14 years. The two biggest unions, UGT and CCOO, called a 24-hour walkout over rents and pay. It landed less than three weeks before the snap election on 29 November. Unions said about 4 million people took part. The employers' group CEOE called it a flop, with activity down only around 5%. Both sides did agree on one thing: factories and transport stopped far more than shops and bars.

How a strike gets counted: nobody actually counts. Spanish workers don't have to tell anyone in advance that they are striking. So everyone reaches for a proxy, and the favourite is the plug socket. Red Eléctrica, the grid operator, publishes a forecast of what a normal weekday would use, and you compare the real demand with it. At the peak hour, between 10 and 11am, demand was as much as 15.5% below forecast. Averaged over the whole day, the gap was much smaller. Unions quote the peak. Bosses quote the average. Both are reading the same chart.

Why it matters
Housing is the issue of this Spanish election, and the strike was a test of how angry people really are. If you work on the Costa del Sol, the same squeeze shows up in rents and staff turnover. And next time a strike's size is in dispute, ask which hour of the electricity chart is being quoted.

Medicine · recent research

Your waist may say more than your BMI

BMI is medicine's bathroom scale: quick, cheap and a bit blunt. A study in Nature this week (11 November) tested a newer definition of obesity on 844,108 adults in China, the UK, the US and Spain. It adds a waist measure to BMI. Then it asks a second question: is the extra fat actually harming the body? If a person has one of 18 related problems, such as sleep apnoea or heart failure, that counts as clinical obesity. If not, it is preclinical.

Only about a quarter to a third of obesity (23–31%) was clinical. That group had a 69% higher risk of death than people without obesity. The preclinical group's risk was just 19% higher, and 77–91% of them had not progressed to clinical obesity ten years later. The twist: obesity flagged by BMI alone, with no waist measure to back it up, was not linked to a higher risk of death. The limits: this is observational data, partly from medical records. It cannot prove the fat caused the organ problems, and the cohorts lean middle-aged and older.

Why it matters
A number on the scale is not the same as a diagnosis, and a tape measure adds real information. Per the study, BMI alone missed obesity-level body fat in up to 8.6% of people and wrongly labelled up to 3.1%. General information, not medical advice.

Art & History · explained

When artists painted warships to lie

In 1917, with U-boats sinking British merchant ships at an alarming rate, the marine painter Norman Wilkinson had a contrary idea. You cannot hide a ship on the open sea, so why try? Instead, cover it in clashing stripes and curves, so a submarine commander peering through a periscope misjudges its course. Dazzle was born.

Artists did the work. Wilkinson's team tested designs on model ships in the basement of the Royal Academy, then sent plans to ports around Britain. The Vorticist Edward Wadsworth oversaw the painting in Bristol and Liverpool, and later turned it into a huge canvas, Dazzle-ships in Drydock at Liverpool. The US Navy took up the idea in 1918. Picasso, spotting a camouflaged cannon in Paris, is said to have told Gertrude Stein: "It is we who made that." Cubism had joined up.

Did it work? Even in 1918, an Admiralty committee could find no clear proof that dazzle cut sinkings, yet advised keeping it because crews felt safer. Recent lab studies from Aston and Abertay universities agree the effect was real but modest: the stripes twist a ship's apparent heading by around 10 degrees, and a horizon illusion did more of the fooling.

Why it matters
Dazzle swapped the question "can they see me?" for "can they read me?" Sometimes confusing an enemy is cheaper than vanishing, and a boost to morale can count as a result even when the data stays murky.

Science · explained

The kids' sum maths still can't crack

Pick any whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, triple it and add one. Repeat. The Collatz conjecture, dating from the 1930s, says you will always land on 1. Try 6: 3, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Easy. Now try 27. It takes 111 steps, soaring to 9,232 before crashing down. The ups and downs earn these sequences the nickname "hailstone numbers".

Computers have checked every starting number below 2⁷¹, roughly 2.4 sextillion, and every one falls to 1 (David Barina, published 2025). But checking is not proving: a rogue number could lurk further out. In 2019 Terence Tao showed that almost all starting numbers drop to almost bounded values. It was the strongest result in decades, yet it still leaves room for exceptions.

Why so stubborn? The rule mixes multiplying and halving in a way that looks random but isn't, and no known tool tames both at once. Paul Erdős reportedly remarked that mathematics was "not yet ready for such problems". Nearly a century after it was posed, it still seems not to be.

Why it matters
How simply a problem is stated tells you little about how hard it is. Some of the deepest questions hide inside rules a child can follow.

Mental Model · explained

Paradigm shift: when the rules themselves change

In 1962 the physicist-turned-historian Thomas Kuhn published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. His claim: science doesn't just pile up facts. Most of the time scientists do "normal science", solving puzzles inside a shared framework he called a paradigm. Then anomalies turn up. At first they get patched or ignored. When enough pile up, you get a crisis, and eventually a revolution.

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912 and was largely dismissed: he had no mechanism. Only in the 1960s, with new evidence from the seafloor, did plate tectonics become the consensus. Wegener had died in 1930. Kuhn's spikier point was that rival paradigms use different concepts and standards, so you can't simply score them against each other. A paradigm shift isn't a better answer to the old question; it changes what counts as a question.

Then the phrase escaped into the boardroom, where it now means any new app. Kuhn meant something rarer and slower. Max Planck put the human side bluntly: "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die." The snappier "science advances one funeral at a time" is a later paraphrase.

Why it matters
Anomalies are information, not noise. When the facts that don't fit keep piling up, the problem may be the framework, not the facts. Worth noticing before someone younger does it for you.

Philosophy
“The human understanding when it has once adopted an opinion (either as being the received opinion or as being agreeable to itself) draws all things else to support and agree with it.”
Francis Bacon, Novum Organum, Book I, Aphorism XLVI · 1620 · trans. James Spedding, Robert Leslie Ellis & Douglas Denon Heath

Bacon wrote this in 1620, in the part of Novum Organum about the "idols" that distort our thinking. He goes on: even when the contrary evidence is greater in number and weight, the mind neglects it, despises it, or invents a distinction to set it aside, all to keep its earlier conclusions safe. It is confirmation bias, described more than three centuries before psychologists named it. Read next to Kuhn, it explains why paradigms are so sticky: the old view doesn't just sit there, it actively recruits evidence to its side.

Book of the week · Fridays

The Invention of Nature, by Andrea Wulf

Before Darwin, and before anyone had coined the word 'ecology', there was Alexander von Humboldt: a restless Prussian who hauled barometers up the Andes and came down seeing nature as one connected web. Andrea Wulf's 2015 biography follows him through rainforests, Paris salons and Jefferson's White House, then traces his ideas into Darwin, Thoreau and John Muir. The best scene: after climbing Chimborazo in 1802, he begins sketching a cross-section of the mountain with its plants stacked by altitude, basically an infographic, two centuries early. It won the Costa Biography Award and the Royal Society's science book prize, and it reads like an adventure story.

Read it if
you've ever wondered why a squid, a penguin, an ocean current and a California county are all named after the same man.
