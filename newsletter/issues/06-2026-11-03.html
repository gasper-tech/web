Markets & Macro · this week

Japan's 43-year plan to sell its stocks

On Friday the Bank of Japan kept its interest rate at 1.25%, as everyone expected. The real news was in the small print. For years the bank bought Japanese stock funds to prop up the market, and it now sits on a huge pile: about ¥37 trillion at purchase price, and worth more than twice that today. Its original plan was to sell ¥330 billion a year, a pace that would have taken about 112 years. From January 2027 it will sell ¥860 billion a year instead. The new estimated finish line is roughly 43 years away.

How a central bank unwinds: When you own a big slice of a market, you can't just dump it. Prices would tumble, and the bank would hurt the economy it is meant to protect. So it sells in drips sized to the market: about 0.1% of trading volume, up from 0.05%, with the option to pause if markets wobble. Investors shrugged, which is exactly what the bank wanted. Emergency buying can take a few years; undoing it can take decades.

Why it matters
The ECB also bought trillions of euros of bonds in past crises and is unwinding them slowly. Bonds at least get repaid on a fixed date; shares never mature, so Japan has to sell every last one itself. Crisis tools can hang around long after the crisis ends.

The UN mission that ended by running out of time

For 35 years, a small UN team watched over a ceasefire in Western Sahara, a desert territory on Africa's Atlantic coast. Morocco claims it; so does the Algeria-backed Polisario Front. The mission, known as MINURSO, was also meant to organise a referendum on the territory's future. That vote never happened. On 30 October the Security Council weighed two rival plans to extend it. A US draft, which leaned towards Morocco's autonomy plan, won 11 votes but was vetoed by Russia. A Russian draft won only three. So at midnight on 31 October, the mission simply expired. Morocco cheered.

How UN mandates work: Peacekeeping missions don't run forever. The Security Council gives each one a mandate with an end date, often a year, and has to vote again to keep it alive. A resolution needs nine of the 15 votes and no 'no' from any of the five permanent members: the US, UK, France, China and Russia. Abstaining is not a veto. The default is off: if the Council can't agree, the mission just ends.

Why it matters
Western Sahara is Europe's near neighbour and a recurring headache in EU–Morocco trade and migration deals. A single veto switched off a 35-year arrangement without anyone voting to end it. The dispute itself is still unresolved, and a UN envoy is still working on it.

Medicine · recent research

Tai chi takes on the gold-standard insomnia therapy

If you've ever stared at the ceiling at 3am, here's a gentle surprise. In a randomised trial published in The BMJ in November 2025, University of Hong Kong researchers split 200 adults aged 50 and over with chronic insomnia into two groups. One got CBT-I, the talking therapy doctors recommend first for insomnia. The other got tai chi. Both met for one-hour group classes twice a week for three months. Straight after the course, CBT-I did clearly better. A year later the gap had almost gone: at 15 months, tai chi was about as good as CBT-I. Insomnia scores had fallen 9.51 points with tai chi and 10.18 with CBT-I, a difference of just 0.68.

The trial was well run, but it has limits. It took place at a single site in Hong Kong, everyone was 50 or older, and people obviously knew which group they were in. The authors also say the lasting benefit may partly come from people who kept practising tai chi. And it says nothing about people who already sleep well.

Why it matters
CBT-I is the best-proven treatment for chronic insomnia, but it can be hard to get. A cheap, gentle group exercise that catches up with it over time is a useful extra option, and it pays off over months, not in a week. Not medical advice: ongoing insomnia is worth raising with a doctor.

Sources: Siu et al., The BMJ (2025) · BMJ Group press release · HealthDay: Tai chi for long-term insomnia

History · explained

'We are hungry': Egypt's first recorded strike

The men who cut and painted the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings were not slaves. They were skilled state employees who lived with their families in a walled village now called Deir el-Medina. They were paid in grain, which was both their wages and their groceries. So when the grain stopped arriving, people noticed fast.

In the 29th year of Ramesses III's reign, around 1157 BCE, the deliveries were badly late. The crew put down their tools, walked past the guard posts and sat down behind a royal temple. A papyrus records their complaint in three words: "We are hungry." It is the earliest known record of a labour strike, written long before anyone had a word for it.

It worked, for a while. Officials found grain, the men went back to work, and then they walked out again when the next payment was late. We know because a tomb scribe called Amennakhte wrote it down. His report, the Turin Strike Papyrus, is now in the Museo Egizio in Turin. It reads less like a royal epic and more like the minutes of a very tense meeting.

Why it matters
The tomb builders were workers with leverage, not faceless slaves. And a state struggling to pay its own people is an early crack in a superpower.

Science · explained

Old windows don't drip: glass isn't a slow liquid

You've probably heard it on a church tour. Old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass is secretly a very slow liquid that has sagged over the centuries. It's a lovely story, and it's wrong.

In 2017, scientists at Corning (the company behind Gorilla Glass) worked out how runny real medieval glass is at room temperature, based on the recipe of 13th-century Westminster Abbey windows. Their answer: such glass would flow roughly one nanometre in a billion years. A nanometre is about the width of a few atoms. Since the Middle Ages, the sag is far too small to see with any ordinary instrument.

So why the lumpy panes? Blame how they were made. Medieval glassmakers often blew a bubble of molten glass and spun it into a flat disc, called crown glass. The disc came out thicker in the middle than at the rim, so every pane cut from it was a bit uneven. Glaziers usually put the heavy edge at the bottom, because it sat more steadily. Every so often a pane went in thick side up, which is the giveaway.

Why it matters
A 'scientific' fact spread because it sounded clever, when the real answer was in the workshop. Glass is an amorphous solid: disordered like a liquid, rigid like a rock.

More: Penn State News: Westminster Abbey windows and the glass myth · University of Cambridge DoITPoMS: The glass myth

Mental Model · explained

Heuristics: the brain's useful shortcuts

A heuristic is a mental shortcut: a good-enough answer, fast, without doing the full sum. In 1974 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a short paper in Science that named three of them. It became one of the most cited papers in social science.

Availability: we judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind. Asked whether more English words start with K or have K as the third letter, most people say "start with". It's the other way round; words starting with K are just easier to recall. Representativeness: we judge by resemblance. Describe Steve as shy, tidy and fond of detail, and people guess "librarian" over "farmer", forgetting there are far more farmers. Anchoring: a starting number pulls our estimate towards it. People spun a wheel rigged to stop at 10 or 65, then guessed the share of African countries in the UN. The median guesses were 25% and 45%. The first price on a tag works the same way.

Gerd Gigerenzer pushed back. He argued that heuristics are often not bugs but well-made tools, "fast and frugal" rules that use little information and still do well in a messy world. In one study, German students beat American students at guessing whether San Diego or San Antonio was bigger. They simply went with the city they had heard of.

Why it matters
Shortcuts run most of our everyday judgements. Knowing when they help and when they mislead is the real skill.

More: Tversky & Kahneman, Science (1974) · Gigerenzer & Goldstein, Psychological Review (1996) · Goldstein & Gigerenzer, Psychological Review (2002)

Philosophy
“We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.”
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, ch. 1 · c. 340 BC · trans. W. D. Ross

Aristotle had a plain answer to how you become a good person: practise. Builders learn by building, lyre-players by playing, and the rest of us learn courage and fairness by acting fairly and bravely, again and again, until it stops feeling like effort. Character, for him, was not a gift you were born with. Virtue is a habit before it is a feeling. That's quietly hopeful. You don't have to feel brave first. You do the brave thing, and the feeling follows, a bit like breaking in a good pair of shoes.
