The oil reserve that buys time, not oil

On Thursday the International Energy Agency (IEA), the rich countries' energy club, said members will speed up the rest of their emergency oil release, including more than 20 million barrels of diesel "as soon as possible". In March they pledged a record 400 million barrels, and about 330 million have already gone out. Brent crude stayed above $100 a barrel. Analysts mostly called it old barrels delivered faster, not new ones.

Stock vs flow: a strategic reserve is a pantry. The Strait of Hormuz is the daily delivery truck, which in peacetime carries roughly 20 million barrels a day. The 100 million barrels still to come cover about five days of that truck. A pantry can get you through a bad week, but it can't replace the truck. And markets price news early: by the time the meeting confirmed what traders expected, it was already in the price.

Why it matters
Energy pushed euro-area inflation to 3.8% in September, and it lands in your fuel and heating bills. When politicians announce a fix, ask: is it a one-off stock or a lasting flow, and is it actually new?

Business · this week

China's export machine hits a new gear

On Wednesday, China's customs office reported that September exports jumped 36.3% from a year earlier, to about $447 billion. Economists had expected roughly 25%. The surplus for January to September reached $938 billion, closing in on last year's full-year record of about $1.19 trillion. The standout was chips: exports up 136% to almost $45 billion.

The twist: China barely shipped more chips. The number of units rose just 1%. Nearly all the jump came from price, because AI data centres are hoovering up memory chips and causing a shortage. China isn't only selling more. It's selling the same things at boom-time prices. Europe feels it: China's surplus with the EU hit $34.8 billion in September alone, a week after Brussels persuaded Beijing to halve its hybrid-car exports to Europe over four years.

Why it matters
One country's surplus is everyone else's deficit. When the world's factory sells far more than it buys, its partners feel it in their own factories and jobs, and answer with quotas and tariffs. Expect more Europe-vs-China deals like the hybrid one.

Sources: CNBC · BNN Bloomberg · CNBC on the hybrid deal

The idea: Most of your inbox is noise: newsletters, receipts, "just following up". Instead of scrolling through it all, let AI read the last 24 hours of Gmail and hand you a short morning note in three parts: reply today, worth a look and safe to ignore. You stop checking email to find out whether anything matters, and only open it when something does.

How to build it:

• Simplest: connect Gmail to Claude (Settings → Connectors, works on the free plan) and ask: "Read my emails from the last 24 hours. Group them into reply today, worth a look and ignore, with one line each." Tweak the wording until the sorting feels right.
• Automated: save that prompt as a daily 7am scheduled task in Claude. It needs a paid plan (Pro is $20 a month) and runs in the cloud even when your laptop is closed.
• Next level: let it add Gmail labels and write draft replies for the "reply today" pile. It never sends anything on its own: you check each draft and press send.

Could you sell it?
Not as a general tool: SaneBox has sent daily inbox digests for years, and AI email apps like Shortwave already summarise and triage. The opening is a narrow group with one messy kind of inbox, like parents buried in school emails or small landlords juggling tenant messages. You'd sell a well-tuned setup for that niche, not new software.

Forget 10,000 steps. About 7,000 does most of the work

The 10,000-step goal is usually traced to a 1960s Japanese pedometer ad, not to science. In 2025 a team led by Melody Ding at the University of Sydney pooled 57 studies of adults whose steps were counted by a device. Compared with people taking about 2,000 steps a day, those taking about 7,000 had a 47% lower risk of dying during follow-up, and a 38% lower risk of dementia.

For death, heart disease, dementia and falls, the benefit rose steeply and then flattened at around 5,000–7,000 steps a day. More didn't hurt, but the big gains came early. One caveat: these are observational studies, and healthier people may simply walk more, so it shows a link, not proof.

Why it matters
If 10,000 feels out of reach, don't give up. Most of the payoff comes from getting off the couch, from about 2,000 steps to 5,000–7,000. Two short daily walks get most people there.

Sources: Ding et al., The Lancet Public Health 2025 · NPR coverage

History · explained

How a coffee house became Lloyd's of London

In 1688 a London newspaper told readers to report stolen watches to "Mr. Edward Loyd's Coffee House" on Tower Street. It's the first record of the world's most famous insurance market. Lloyd's trick was simple: next to the coffee, he served shipping news. Which ships had docked, which were overdue, who was sailing where. Captains and merchants came for the gossip and stayed to do business.

The deals worked like this. A shipowner wrote the voyage and cargo on a slip of paper. Wealthy men read it over their coffee, and each who wanted a share of the risk signed his name beneath it for a cut of the premium. Signing underneath made them underwriters. Lloyd sold the coffee and the news, and other people built an industry around his tables.

In 1769 the serious underwriters split off, tired of gamblers betting on wars and on how long famous people would live. Parliament made Lloyd's a corporation in 1871. It kept the coffee-house name.

Why it matters
Markets grow up around good information. Lloyd built neither a bank nor an insurer. He built the place everyone needed to be.

Science · explained

The Moon is leaving us, about as fast as your fingernails grow

The Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon. Observatories still fire lasers at them and time how long the light takes to come back. The result: the Moon drifts about 3.8 cm farther from Earth every year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow.

The cause is the tides. The Moon's gravity raises a bulge in Earth's oceans, and because Earth spins much faster than the Moon orbits, the spin carries that bulge slightly ahead of the Moon. The bulge tugs the Moon forward into a higher orbit, while the Moon pulls back on it like a brake. Whatever spin Earth loses, the Moon gains as orbit, so our days get longer while the Moon drifts away. 1.4 billion years ago, a day lasted just over 18 hours. No need to panic: in a lifetime the Moon moves about three metres.

Why it matters
Nothing in the sky is truly fixed. Small, steady forces add up hugely over enough time, in orbits and in life.

Mental Model · explained

Goodhart's law: the day Hanoi paid for rat tails

In 1902, French colonial Hanoi had a rat problem and feared plague. Officials offered a bounty of about one cent per rat, and to avoid piles of dead rats in their offices, they asked only for the tail. On one day in June, more than 20,000 tails came in. A triumph? Soon tailless rats were seen scurrying around the city: people were cutting off tails and letting the rats keep breeding. Some reportedly started farming them.

Economist Charles Goodhart spotted the pattern in 1975: any statistical regularity tends to collapse once you lean on it for control. The catchy version came later, from anthropologist Marilyn Strathern: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Why it matters
Every number stands in for something you actually care about. Reward the number and people get good at moving the number. Before you set a target, ask: how could someone hit this without doing the real thing?

Philosophy
“In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book V, §1 · c. AD 170–180 · tr. George Long

Marcus Aurelius was emperor of Rome, the most powerful man alive, and he still struggled to get out of bed. He wrote the Meditations as a private notebook, probably on campaign along the Danube, never meant for anyone else. In the same passage he scolds himself: was he made "to lie in the bedclothes and keep myself warm?" The bees and ants get on with their work, so why can't he? When the duvet wins the argument, ask what you were actually made for, then go and do one piece of it.

Book of the week · Fridays

The Righteous Mind, by Jonathan Haidt

Published in 2012, Jonathan Haidt's book asks why good, smart people look at the same facts and end up furious with each other about politics and religion. His answer: intuition decides first, and reasoning arrives afterwards like a press secretary to defend the decision. In his image, reason is a small rider on a large elephant. He also shows that morality covers more than harm and fairness: loyalty, authority, liberty and sanctity matter too, and different groups weigh them differently.

Read it if
you want to argue less and understand more, especially with people who vote differently from you. Read Part I slowly. The later chapters on evolution are the most debated, so take them with a pinch of salt.
